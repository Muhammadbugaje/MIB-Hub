#### M025 · Stalite
**When:** Y2 · Freshers' Week · Mon (resumption day) · 10:00–16:00. **Where:** hostel: your room, stairs, lodge, storeroom. **Who:** {{ROOMIE}}, Musa B. (a fresher), Baba Porter, Muhammad.
**Connects to:** M1 (you were the fresher on that bed); M22 (Baba Porter's "thirty years"); M24 ("Five more" is still on the note in your drawer).
**Mystery:** plants C-606 (post for a room on a floor that does not exist), MS-08 (a fresher is sent to you by someone he cannot then remember).

**Story.** You come back from the long vacation heavier by one bag of your mother's foodstuff and lighter by most of your father's patience. The campus has kept its promise from convocation night: the Senate is freshly painted, there are lamps along the ring road that work, and a second shuttle. The fourth floor has not been painted. Some things are permanent.

Your key works. Your door opens. On your bed there is a boy. He is asleep with his shoes on, one arm round a Ghana-must-go bag, and chalked on the bed frame above his head, in a clerk's capitals, is a name that is not yours: MUSA B.

{{ROOMIE}}, arriving behind you with a mattress on their head, takes in the scene. "Riddle me this. What has two owners and one mattress?" A pause. "No, I'm actually asking. What do we do?"

You are a "stalite" now, a returning student, and the game lets you feel what a year has given you. Musa wakes and is terrified of you, which is new. You know which tap runs, which stair is loose, what KB charges for bread and what he charges *you*. The Registry has double-allocated the bed, as it does every year to somebody; the fix is not a fight but a favour economy. Share the room for a week while it is sorted, or find Musa a mattress and a corner today, through three people who owe you something from last year: Bolaji knows a man with a spare mattress, Nusaiba knows which hall officer signs transfers, Mubarak knows a guy. Each debt you call in is a callback.

{{ROOMIE}} mediates with a riddle and with your garri, both without asking.

At the lodge, Baba Porter is sorting the vacation's post into pigeonholes. One small pile he does not sort. He puts it in a biscuit tin with a picture of a Scottish castle on the lid. You can read the top envelope before the lid goes on: no stamp, a careful hand, **Room 606**. Giginya has five floors. You have climbed all of them carrying water. "There's no sixth floor," you say. "No," he agrees, and puts the tin on a high shelf, and asks whether you have signed for your key.

Down in the storeroom of forgotten things, which he opens for stalites only, a year of abandoned property is yours to pick through: this is the loot room, refilled weekly from now on.

When it is settled, Musa stands at your door with the gratitude of a boy who expected to sleep in a corridor. "The man on the stairs said you'd help. He said, 'Fourth floor, the one who chased the goat. Don't be afraid of them.'" Which man? "He was just… on the stairs. White shirt. He said sorry for something." Musa turns to point. The stairwell is empty in both directions. His hand comes down slowly. "There was… I think there was a man." He is no longer sure. By evening, when you ask again, he says nobody sent him; he just knocked.

#### M026 · Grand Opening
**When:** Y2 · S1 Wk 2 · Sat (weekend) · 09:00–20:00. **Where:** market; mall: atrium, escalator. **Who:** Hajiya Ladi, Chief Megawatt, Mubarak, KB, Aisha, traders. **Special helpers (if enabled):** Madison.
**Connects to:** M10 (Hajiya Ladi and "bring your book"); M23 (the sticker on Aliyu's van: KWARI MALL, OPENING SOON); M20 (plaque two: "Beneath the market, where people gather").
**Mystery:** plants the mall (always lit), Megawatt ("somebody always pays"), C-KB (his new shop, facing the mall, with batteries already on the top shelf).

**Story.** Over the long vacation the eastern half of Gidan Kwari market has been swallowed by a building. It is glass, it is three storeys, it has a fountain indoors and a name in letters taller than a man: KWARI MALL. The traders who used to sit where it stands have been offered stalls inside. The stalls are a third the size and three times the rent, and the offer expires at closing time today.

Hajiya Ladi has decided the traders will take them, for now, because a stall you hate is still a stall. She has also decided that you are helping. It is a carrying and stacking job against the clock: sacks of rice, bolts of cloth, a tower of enamel basins, forty traders' goods through one service door before the ribbon is cut at four. Her daughter Aisha, sixteen and sharper than her mother admits, keeps the tally. The traders you help today will remember it for four years.

The mall has an escalator. It is the first escalator anyone in {{TOWN}} has ever seen, and it only goes up. Nobody has found the one that goes down. An elderly trader rides it for the third time with her basin on her head, serene, and asks from the top: "How do I come down? Is it a different building?"

At four a brass band plays slightly out of tune and Chief Megawatt cuts the ribbon with scissors as long as his arm. He is a big, warm, well-dressed man who remembers your name the second time he hears it and gives every student present a free torch. The torch has his face on it. "A gift! So you will think of me in the dark." He laughs. Everyone laughs. You look at the torch.

Across the road, in a kiosk that used to sell recharge cards, a new sign: KB & SONS (NO SONS YET). KB has moved his blackboard to face the mall's front door. "They have price tags. Printed. Imagine. A price that doesn't know who it's talking to." Batteries are on his top shelf, at the front. They are not cheap today.

Mubarak has stood in the atrium for an hour with his mouth open, doing sums.

You walk home at dusk. And it is on the hostel road, looking back, that you see the thing the mission exists to show you. The campus, with its one lit coil and its new lamps, is dim tonight: brown, patchy, a bulb here and there. And behind you, in the middle of it, the mall is blazing like a ship. Every window. The sign. The fountain lit from below.

Megawatt's car pulls up beside you; he has seen you looking. The window comes down. "Beautiful, isn't it? You are thinking: why is his so bright?" He is entirely friendly. "Light is not free, my friend. It was never free. Somebody always pays. I am simply honest about it." The window goes up.

#### M027 · Plug and Play
**When:** Y2 · S1 Wk 3 · Thu (market day) · 14:00–19:00. **Where:** student: SUG; market: motor park, main. **Who:** Mubarak, Hajiya Ladi, Nusaiba, a supplier ("Oga Sharp"). **Special helpers (if enabled):** Madison.
**Connects to:** M10 (the locker, "I know a guy", "bring your book"); M26 (the mall has given him ideas); M4 (GUCCY).
**Mystery:** plants C-PADLOCK (the first carton with the Plug's sticker; a second buyer at the same supplier, in good shoes), M45 (the power banks are warm).

**Story.** The Plug has come up in the world: a real stall in the Student Union arcade, between the photocopier and a woman who sells boiled eggs. A sign-painter is adding STORE after THE PLUG, runs out of board, and finishes it vertically down the side, so that it reads, depending on your angle, THE PLUG STORE or THE PLUG S T O R E like a cry for help. Mubarak is delighted. "It's distinctive."

What he does not have is stock. The mall sells everything he sells, with a receipt. His answer is volume. He knows a guy.

The guy is at the motor park, behind the tyre menders, in a container with a fan: a soft-voiced man everybody calls Oga Sharp. He sells power banks by the carton at a price that makes Mubarak's hands shake. Also padlocks, torches, phone chargers "original, almost". The bargaining is in three rounds and it is the fullest version of the system yet: your Street sense reads his tells, your Charm softens the round, and walking away is a move. You can also put in ₦5,000 of your own for a share of the Plug; the game is clear that this is a real stake, and that the story will pay it back or not.

While you bargain, another customer waits his turn at the container door: a man in very good shoes, not a student, not a trader. He is here for padlocks. Two hundred. Oga Sharp says he will have them next term. The man says next term is fine; he is in no hurry at all. You think nothing of it.

Nusaiba has come because Mubarak asked her to "glance at something". The something is Oga Sharp's contract, one page, which Mubarak has already signed. She reads it in ten seconds. She turns it over. The back is blank. "Where is the warranty?" "It's understood." "Where is the *supplier's name*?" "It's Oga Sharp." "That is not a name. That is a review." She clicks her pen for a long time. "Mubarak, when this goes wrong, and I'm saying when, I am not saying I told you so. I am saying it now, to save time."

Hajiya Ladi, passing with her ledger, looks at the cartons going on to your keke. She does not say anything. She lifts one power bank out, holds it in her palm for a moment as you would hold an egg to see if it is fresh, and puts it back.

You notice it too, loading the last carton. Each one is very slightly warm. Nobody has plugged them in.

That evening the Plug Store sells forty power banks in an hour, at a price the mall cannot match, to students who are sick of blackouts. Mubarak sticks the Plug's new sticker, a little yellow lightning bolt, on every single thing in the shop, including the egg-seller's tray. He texts you a photograph of money fanned out like a hand of cards. "PRE-RICH NO MORE."

**Connects to:** M29 (the kerb plate); M30 (the lever); M27 (the Plug's stickers; Oga Sharp's cartons); M26 (the escalator).

**Story.** The cable from the kerb plate goes down. So you are going down.

Mubarak "lends" you a Ghost Hoodie for the purpose. It is grey, it smells of the container, and for ten minutes by the timer on your screen, security does not seem able to settle its eyes on you. It is the first time he has given you something without being asked and without naming a price. "Don't thank me. If you're caught, you don't know me. If you're not caught, tell people where you got it."

The mall after closing is the game's first proper stealth level, and it is funnier than it is frightening. Guards patrol in pairs and gossip. The escalator has been switched off, which makes it a staircase. {{ROOMIE}}, at the top of it, in a whisper of real wonder: "So it *can* come down." In the bedding department you hide inside a display of mattresses while a guard tests one, thoroughly, for a quarter of an hour, and takes a phone call from his wife lying on it.

The service stair goes down two flights past the car park and then keeps going, through a door that is older than the building: plank and iron. Below is what the mall was built on top of. The old market's cellar: brick vaults, a floor of beaten earth, hooks in the ceiling where meat used to hang. It is warm down here, and it smells of diesel.

She is in the largest vault. A generator the size of a bus, squatting on railway sleepers, painted in yellow gloss by somebody proud of her: **IRON MAMA**. She is not running the mall off diesel; her tank is nearly dry. A cable as thick as your leg runs from her into the floor, and at the place where it goes in there is a ring of brick, and in the ring, grey with dust, a coil. The twin of the one in the garden. Dark for forty years. She is drinking from it, and through it, from everything it is joined to.

Round her feet, something moves. Shades, but changed: each has a chest like a bucket of loose bolts, and rattles. They do not drift toward you. They sit on the cables, like hens. Step on a cable and one charges, in a dead straight line, and takes you off your feet; you sit down hard with stars round your head and get up. Step aside and it goes past, into the wall. Hummers.

On the brickwork, under a tear-off calendar that stopped in a year before your parents married, a brass plaque: "Beneath the market, where people gather." Round the dead coil's rim, the same ten empty sockets.

You call the only person who would want to know. He answers on the second ring, as if he had never been anywhere. You describe it. There is a pause, and then, in the voice of a man permitting himself one small pleasure: "A thermodynamically incontinent leviathan, hoarding our electrons."

{{ROOMIE}}, leaning in to the phone: "English, sir?"

"A greedy generator."

Behind her, as you back out, your torch crosses a stack of cartons against the wall. New padlocks, school-pattern, a gross to a carton. Each carton has a small yellow sticker on it, a lightning bolt. You know the sticker. You have stuck two hundred of them on things yourself. You tell yourself that he sells to everybody. You go up the stairs.

#### M034 · Sly Goes Straight
**When:** Y2 · S1 Wk 11 · Sat (weekend) · 10:00–14:00. **Where:** market: Sly's Cuts, Big Baby's salon. **Who:** Bolaji, Big Baby, Hassan, Abbas, Malam Garba, wary customers.

**Story.** There is a new kiosk beside Big Baby's salon. It was a recharge-card booth. It has been scrubbed, painted white by somebody very large with a very small brush, and fitted with one swivel chair, one mirror, and a sign in careful blue letters: **SLY'S CUTS**. Under it, smaller: *Bolaji A., Prop.* It is the first time most of the campus has seen his name.

He is sweeping. The floor is already clean. He has swept it four times since eight. On the bench outside, Hassan and Abbas sit with a pack of score cards from some lost sports day, ready.

Nobody comes. A boy walks up, sees who is holding the clippers, remembers ₦200 and a chalk circle, and discovers he needs to be elsewhere. Another asks through the door, from a safe distance, whether "the big man" will be doing it himself. This is the mission's problem and it is not one you can fight: the man who taxed the court for two years would like you to trust him with a blade near your ears.

You can be first. The game makes it a real offer: the haircut system opens here (styles, each with a Charm value and reactions from people who know you), and the first chair is free. Whoever sits, the same thing is discovered. His hands are the size of bread loaves and they are perfectly gentle. He hums while he works. He turns your head with two fingertips as if it were an egg. Through the wall of the salon next door Big Baby, with a customer of her own under the dryer, is watching in her mirror, which is angled to see his. She taught him. He was ashamed of it for a year, because it was "small work". She does not come in. She lets it be his.

The twist walks in at noon without knocking. A small man in a plain kaftan, with a ledger under his arm even on a Saturday. Bolaji's hands, for the first time, shake. Then they stop. It takes twenty minutes and nobody on the bench makes a sound. Malam Garba's left hand rests palm-up on the chair arm the whole time, and in the palm, faded, as if written and rewritten in ink every day for years, is a word. You are at the wrong angle to read it.

When it is done, he regards himself in the mirror for a long while. "Hm," he says. He pays double. At the door he pauses. "My father was a barber. People think it is small work." He does not look back. "It is the only work where a man must trust you with his head."

By two there is a queue. Bolaji is not sweeping any more.

Number four is yours ("The bed is for sick people; you are only foolish"). Number one is Jega's. She reads the whole list in silence and has to look at the ceiling for a moment.

Zainab opens her eyes. "Of all the places in Nigeria." She goes to the window. "I can see it from here. I can see the *roof*. Aigoo." Nobody in the room is sorry. She looks at you, and for once the roast does not come. "Fine. Somebody has to keep you people alive. Drink water."

At dusk you help carry chairs back to the staff club, a low veranda'd building you have never had reason to enter. Its long wall is hung with class photographs, one a year, back and back. You set the chairs down under the oldest.

It is forty years old. A woman in the middle, laughing at the photographer: the professor. Six students in lab coats round her on six stools. You know one of them at once, though he is twenty: light skin, dark coily hair, the only one not smiling, as if he already suspected. The Maker.

And behind them, half in the doorway of the laboratory, not posed, as if he had been passing and paused: a young man in a plain white shirt, hands folded, looking not at the camera but slightly to one side.

You saw that face last week, at the hearing. It has not aged a day.

Your phone rings in your hand. Unknown number. You answer, still looking at the wall. Nobody speaks. Three notes, slow, clear. Then a woman's voice, amused, and you realise she has begun one number later than she did last year.

"Six."

The line goes dead.
