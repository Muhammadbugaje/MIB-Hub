#### M001 · Welcome, Fresher
**When:** Y1 · Freshers' Week · Mon (resumption day) · 07:00 to sunset. **Where:** gate, hostel, student (the Spot), ring road, agric. **Who:** Mum, Dad, Baba Porter, {{ROOMIE}}, Mama Nkechi, Malam Sule, Sarki the goat. **Special helpers (if enabled):** Taty (a passing bark only).
**Connects to:** nothing earlier. This is the first ten minutes, exactly as bible §3.
**Mystery:** plants C-NOTE (the note), C-SYMBOL (inside the tin, on the note), C-CLOCK (6:06, and one strike), C-HUM (the garden), C-GOAT ("one engineering boy"), MS-01 (a still figure in the film).

**Story.** Dawn over {{UNI_NAME}}: laterite roads, the ring road, the dam like a dropped coin, a clock tower whose hands say 6:06 and mean nothing by it. A family car reaches the Main Gate. Dad is certain this is not the main gate. The guard is certain it is. Mum, in the back, reads from a list that has been growing since the admission letter came: don't follow bad friends, don't follow good friends too closely either, eat, pray, call me.

Baba Porter takes the Ghana-must-go bag on his head with one hand and climbs four flights without changing the rhythm of his complaints. There is no lift. There has never been a lift. Mum makes a bed that is already made. Dad presses folded notes into your palm as if the room were bugged. "Manage it." Then the hug, the door, the car getting small, and Mum's voice arriving after the car has gone: "CALL ME!"

You wake to the sound of your own biscuits. {{ROOMIE}} is on the other bed, guarding them from the inside. A riddle is offered before a name. You are led to the window, which is how you learn to walk and look, and given your kingdom: twin theatres, a clock that is right twice a day ("more than our lecturers"), a line of water beyond.

Downstairs the campus is doing what it does at eight on a Monday: rivers of students toward the faculties, nobody in the road, a queue at the one good tap. At the Spot, Mama Nkechi looks at you once and knows you have not eaten. You pay for two plates because {{ROOMIE}} agreed that you would, in {{ROOMIE}}'s head.

Then a white-faced goat with a brass bell comes through the food court like a rumour, lands on the suya table, and looks you in the eye. It is not a chase so much as a guided insult: it waits at every corner, eats a matriculation poster while you vault a bench, and lets itself be caught only when it is bored. A bicycle leans on a fence ("Borrow it. Everybody does."). Goat in the basket, you ride the ring road with {{ROOMIE}} jogging beside you, narrating badly: Engineering, who don't sleep; the clock, which doesn't work; a fenced garden where something hums. "Don't ask." You ask. "I said don't ask."

At the farm, Malam Sule takes the goat, whose name is Sarki, with the weariness of a man who has done it before. One engineering boy was here, he says, giving it a lecture about littering. Ten minutes. Left the gate open. "You, you brought him back. People don't, you know." He says that last part oddly, as if repeating it for someone. Behind the barn, where the old kola tree was, a loose stone, a tin, a Kola of Vigour. Scratched inside the lid: six dots in a ring and a seventh in the middle.

Sunset. The hostel corridor flickers once. There is a folded note under your door, in careful handwriting, with the same seven dots. "The Maker is watching. Welcome." The corridor is empty. Far off, a clock that does not work strikes once. {{ROOMIE}} reads over your shoulder and, for the only time today, does not make a joke. "…Don't lose that."

#### M002 · Queue Theory
**When:** Y1 · Freshers' Week · Tue · 08:00–16:00. **Where:** admin: Senate, Registry, Bursary, bank. **Who:** Malam Garba, Mrs. Adebayo, Mr. Clement, Aleeya, Muhammad, Mum (phone). **Special helpers (if enabled):** Jiya.
**Connects to:** M1 (the note is in your Pockets; Malam Garba sees it).
**Mystery:** plants C-DOOR7 (a door with no handle), MS-02 (he knows your name; the clerk talks past him), C-CLOCK (the bank's clock also says 6:06: "We set it by the tower").

**Story.** Registration is four offices, four queues and one file, and the file must be stamped in an order that nobody has written down. {{ROOMIE}} sends you off with the only advice anyone gives: "Carry water. Carry a pen. Carry yourself."

The Senate queue curls round the building like a question mark. A tall girl with a kind face holds your bag and your place while you fetch a form, and introduces herself as Aleeya. She compliments your patience and then observes that it is the patience of a goat tied to a post; you like her at once.

At the Registry, Mrs. Adebayo requires four passport photographs. She has required four passport photographs of everyone since 1994. Your phone rings in the silence of the queue: Mum, on loudspeaker by mistake, asking forty strangers whether you have eaten. You learn the phone this way: the call, the first text ("Daddy sent something small. Don't finish it."), the bank alert that follows.

At the Bursary, Mr. Clement says the word. "Network." Forty people sit down on the floor as one, like a prayer. Someone produces a mat. Someone produces ludo.

It is here that a soft-spoken young man in a plain white shirt, whom you had not noticed standing beside you, says: "Excuse me. Sorry. Don't take the shortcut behind the Bursary, {{PLAYER_NAME}}. The gate is locked on Tuesdays." You did not tell him your name. You turn to ask, and Mrs. Adebayo, calling the next person, looks straight through the space where he is standing and says "Next" to the person behind him. He steps aside, apologises to nobody in particular, and by the time the queue has shuffled he is not there.

You can take the shortcut anyway. The game lets you: a narrow lane, a locked gate, a goat, an hour gone, and a security man who wants ₦500 for "trespass on a path". Or you can go round.

The last stamp is in the Senate basement. On the way to Malam Garba's desk you pass a steel door painted with a 7. It has no handle and no keyhole on this side. Malam Garba is a small man with a large ledger. He stamps your file. Then he sees the folded note sticking out of your pocket, and his stamp stops in the air for the length of a breath. He looks at you properly, for the first time. "Hm," he says, and it is a whole paragraph. He writes something in the ledger that is not your name.

Outside, the clock says 6:06. It is four in the afternoon. You are, officially, a student.

**Story.** The night before your first university exam the light does not flicker. It simply stops, district by district; from the hostel roof you watch windows go black in rows, like a hand closing.

Exam week is the worst possible night for it, and that is the idea the mission is built on: everybody is alone. Reading rooms full of people who came precisely to be left in peace, each at a separate desk with a separate candle. From the roof the dark between the buildings looks crowded. It is. The smudges you saw in September are drifting in from the edges of the campus by the dozen, and every one is heading for somebody with a book.

{{ROOMIE}} says it first and it becomes the rule of the night: "One torch is a snack. Five is a problem." So you gather. Block by block, you knock on doors and bring people out into corridors, then down into the quad: a crowd-gathering game in which each group you add makes the light bigger and the next group easier to move. Nusaiba is already doing it on the women's side with a list. Jega arrives with the entire Education team, who have no torches and so bring noise. You ask Bolaji. He is on the court, alone, pretending not to be afraid. He comes. He carries the common room's generator on his shoulder like a handbag.

Here the game switches you for the first time. {{ROOMIE}} stays in the big reading room to keep two hundred students from drifting back to their desks: a rhythm game, call and response, "Up NEPA!", louder each round, while shapes press at the windows. You take the line down the boulevard, because the dark is not wandering any more. It is going somewhere: the Senate.

On the Senate steps it stands up. Hundreds of smudges run together and become one tall thing, and somehow it has a shape you recognise from every cartoon villain you were ever shown: a high collar, a cape. It does not speak. It does not attack. It just stands at the top of the steps and makes the idea of going home to bed feel extremely reasonable.

Forty torches, then eighty. Jega's team singing. Bolaji starts the generator on the bottom step and one string of bulbs comes on. It thins from the edges, like steam, and is gone.

For half a second, at the far end of the boulevard, you see the Maker under a tree, outside his garden, watching you and not the steps. Then the lights come back, all of them at once, and four hundred people who have an exam at eight scream "UP NEPA!" at an empty sky.

In the morning there is a scrap of black cloth caught on the Senate weathervane. It was not there yesterday. Nobody can say who would climb up there, or why it is tied on with a knot.

#### M016 · The Dam at Midnight
**When:** Y1 · S2 Wk 4 · Fri · 23:00–02:00 (night; harmattan haze). **Where:** dam: pier, underwater. **Who:** Baba Dam, {{ROOMIE}}, Mubarak (phone), the eel, Muhammad. **Special helpers (if enabled):** Taty.

**Story.** A text from Baba Dam's nephew's phone, in capitals, because the old man dictated it: "COME. NIGHT. THE WATER IS SINGING. BRING THE LOUD ONE." {{ROOMIE}} is flattered.

Midnight at the dam in harmattan is like standing inside a pearl. The haze has come down so thick that the torch makes a white ball and nothing else. Baba Dam is on the pier with his ear to a post. "Listen." You put your hand on the wood you nailed, and feel it: a note, low, coming up through the planks from the water. The garden's note. Something under the pier is humming the same thing as the coil.

You are going to have to go down, and you learned to float a week ago. Mubarak, phoned at midnight, is awake, because Mubarak is always awake. A boy on a bicycle arrives eleven minutes later with a twist of foil. "Iron Lungs Gum. Free. Trust the Plug. Chew before, not during. If you chew during, don't tell people you bought it from me."

The dive is the game's first, and it is beautiful and a little frightening: green torchlight, your Breath bar tripled and still ticking, the pier's posts going down like the legs of something enormous. On the bottom, half in silt, a brass thing the size of a loaf, with six small dots in a ring cast into its face. It is humming.

And round it, coiled twice, something long and slow with a head like a spade, looking at you with one flat eye. An eel. A very big one. It does not attack. When you reach for the brass it puts its head between your hand and it, firmly, like a clerk.

Baba Dam had told you to bring kilishi, and you had thought it was for him. It is a negotiation: offer, wait, offer again, never grab. The eel takes the dried meat from your fingers with terrible delicacy, considers, and unwinds one coil. Then the other. It watches you lift the brass. It follows you up for a few metres, and then lets you go.

You come up too fast. The gum has run out, the haze has swallowed the pier, and for a bad moment you do not know which way is up; the brass is heavy and you will not let it go. Then a hand closes round your wrist, warm and dry-feeling even in the water, and pulls, and you are lying on planks, coughing, with a towel round your shoulders that smells of sun.

"You'll be all right," someone says, close, gently. "I checked. Sorry."

{{ROOMIE}} comes pounding down the pier with the torch. "Who are you talking to? I heard you talking!" There is nobody on the pier but the two of you and Baba Dam, who is thirty metres away and seventy years old. The towel is real. On the planks, wet footprints lead from where you are lying toward the shore, across the sand, on to the road. In the middle of the road they stop.

#### M024 · Convocation Night
**When:** Y1 · after exams · Sat (event: the seniors' convocation) · 18:00–midnight, then dawn. **Where:** admin: Senate steps, the boulevard; eng: garden. **Who:** everybody who said yes; Malam Garba; Mai Shayi; the Maker; the Dean of Engineering; Muhammad. **Special helpers (if enabled):** Jiya, Taty, Madison.
**Connects to:** M22 (every yes stands in its place, by name); M12 (the Count, taller); M20 (the plaques light; one socket answers); M1 (the note); M23 (the load that the feeders cannot carry).
**Mystery:** pays "what is the Count" (the freshers drew it a face); plants C-NOTE ("Five more."), MS-07 (the Maker sees him and looks away), C-SEALS (the first seal), C-CAPE (still on the vane).

**Story.** The seniors graduate in the afternoon: gowns, mothers in gele the width of doorways, a brass band, speeches. You are an usher, which is how first-years attend. At dusk every light on campus is switched on at once for the convocation dinner: the boulevard strung with bulbs, the Senate floodlit, the fountain running for the only time this year. It is the most beautiful the place has ever looked, and Shamsu, beside you, is doing arithmetic with his lips. "That is too much load. That is all seven feeders. That is—"

The dark comes down like a lid.

Four thousand people in their best clothes. One long indrawn breath. And on the Senate steps, where the Vice-Chancellor was standing a moment ago, something rises that is taller than in December, broad as the doorway, the cape spreading. It has had a whole year of blackouts to eat.

What happens next is the year. Down the boulevard, behind you, they come: not eighty torches, hundreds. Phones held up. Mama Nkechi with a pot and a ladle, banging. Jega and eleven men singing. Bolaji with the generator on a wheelbarrow, Big Baby walking in front to clear the way. Mubarak, selling "I SURVIVED YEAR 1" shirts as he walks, because he is who he is, and giving the torches away, because he is also who he is. Lula and Faty, heckling the dark. Nusaiba with a roster. Mrs. Adebayo, with four photographs of herself, in case.

The fight is at the garden, not the steps. Hold the fence while the coil is brought up to full: waves of smudges from each side, and your friends covering the gaps *by name*, each with the line you earned from them. If someone said no, their gap is yours to hold. The Maker works at the plinth with his sleeves rolled, faster than you have seen him move; and you catch him, once, in the middle of it, looking past you at the crowd. A young man in a white shirt is standing among the torches, holding a lantern up, steady. The Maker's hands stop. Then he looks away, like a man refusing to look at the sun, and bends back to the work.

The coil lights. It is not a flash. It is a note you have heard all year, finally in tune, and a colour that goes out across the campus like water finding its level. Paint looks newer. Lamps you did not know existed come on along the ring road. The thing on the steps is not there; nobody saw it go.

Then music, and four thousand people remembering they are at a party.

Mai Shayi finds you at the edge of it with two cups. "You know who gave that thing a cape?" He nods at the freshers dancing. "Us. First-years. Every year. Forty years ago it was only dark. But we were scared, so we drew it a face. A thing with a face you can stand against." He drinks. "It's still up there on the weathervane, the cape. Somebody keeps it tied on. I wonder who."

The Dean of Engineering presses a brass disc into your palm: coin-sized, six dots and a seventh. "It has been my paperweight. And the last Dean's. Somebody once told us to hand it over 'when your people are ready'. I think that was tonight." You try it in the first of the ten sockets round the coil's foot. It clicks home, the socket glows, and then it lets go again, warm, as if it has only been measured. The Maker does not look up. "Keep it."

Malam Garba, at the Senate door, in his one good kaftan: "Go and dance. Fresher no more."

You get back to your room at dawn. The note is in the drawer where you have kept it since September. You unfold it, for no reason. Under "The Maker is watching. Welcome." there is a second line, in the same careful hand, that was not there yesterday.

**Five more.**
