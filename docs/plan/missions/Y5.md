#### M097 · Finalist
**When:** Y5 · Freshers' Week · Mon (resumption day) · 07:00–19:00. **Where:** gate: main; hostel: stairs, lodge, room. **Who:** Baba Porter, {{ROOMIE}}, three freshers, Muhammad. **Special helpers (if enabled):** none.
**Connects to:** M1 (the note, the goat, somebody else's mother with a list); M2 ("Don't take the shortcut"); M22 (thirty years of freshers); M24 ("Five more"); M25 (post for Room 606).
**Mystery:** pays C-NOTE (who wrote it); plants C-NOTE again ("I only ever write one line"); plants C-606 (the tin of unstamped envelopes); MS-23 (a mattress that "floats").

**Story.** The gate at dawn looks exactly as it did four years ago, except that this time you are leaning on it. A family car arrives, overloaded, and a mother gets out holding a list. It is not your mother. It is the same list.

A fresher with a bag bigger than his future asks you the way to the Senate. You hear your own mouth say, "Don't take the shortcut," and you stop, because you know where you heard it first. {{ROOMIE}}, eating your groundnuts: "Look at you. Senior. Giving directions like a signboard."

Help three freshers, each a small echo of your own first week. One has a bag with a broken wheel: carry it. One is at the back of the Registry queue with three passports instead of four: you have a spare, because you have had a spare since Year 1. One has been allocated a room and found a stranger asleep in the bed, which is where you came in.

The third fresher's mattress is the trouble. The stairs of your hall are narrow and he is carrying it like a man wrestling a crocodile. You go to get the other end and it is already moving: up the stairs, steadily, with the fresher walking behind it open-mouthed. "Senior! It's floating!" From where you stand you can see a white sleeve, a careful hand, and shoes with no dust on them. At the landing the mattress leans itself against the wall. Nobody is there. Baba Porter, from the lodge door, without looking up: "Good boy, that one. Always carries from the heavy end."

Then Baba Porter, whose knees have finally filed a complaint against the stairs, asks you to help him move his lodge to the ground floor. Six boxes, a kettle, a chair nobody else may sit in, and the key rack. Behind the key rack are two biscuit tins. The first is full of unstamped envelopes addressed to Room 606, one a semester, the same hand, decades of them. He takes that tin from you gently and puts it on the high shelf. The second he lets you open.

Notes. Dozens. The same careful hand, the same words, the same symbol of six dots and a seventh. *The Maker is watching. Welcome.* Each has a room number and a year. Most have a tick. Some do not.

"The professor asked me. 'Pick the one who runs toward the trouble.' Every year I pick one door." He taps the unticked ones. "Some years nobody ran. Those I kept." He looks at you over his glasses. "You chased a goat. I had my doubts. But you brought it *back*."

So it was never the Maker. It was a porter with a biscuit tin and an instruction forty years old. You are still holding your own note; you have carried it for four years. You turn it over and ask the question that has been waiting there since convocation night of your first year: who wrote "Five more" on the back?

Baba Porter frowns at it for a long time. "I wrote one line," he says. "I only ever write one line."

#### M098 · The Maker's Notebook
**When:** Y5 · S1 Wk 2 · Tue–Thu · lecture days, office and library hours. **Where:** eng: garden, Dr. Okonkwo's office; lib: rare books room. **Who:** Dr. Okonkwo, the Maker, Mrs. Shehu, Shamsu, Maryam. **Special helpers (if enabled):** none.

**Mystery:** pays C-PAGE41 (what was on it); plants C-SEVENTH ("Keeper. Must be someone who will leave."); plants C-PAGE41 again (whose blade cut it out).

**Story.** Your final-year project title is approved on a Tuesday, in Dr. Okonkwo's handwriting, with one correction to your grammar: "Design and construction of the sixth resonator." She says it the way other supervisors say "a survey of rural water pumps". "It is a real project. You will write a real report. Margins of one inch."

The Maker keeps his promise at the fence. He passes the notebook through the bars with both hands, as you would pass a sleeping child. Then, because it is a major occasion and he cannot help himself: "Pneumonoultramicroscopic patience is required for page two hundred and six." Shamsu, beside you, raises a finger. "Technically, that is a lung disease." "So is waiting. Read."

Page 206 is in a shorthand no student has ever seen. Shamsu fills three sheets with attempts and declares it "a cipher of considerable elegance". Dr. Okonkwo reads it like a newspaper, upside down, across her desk. "It is how lecturers took notes before photocopiers. It says the frame dimensions are wrong on purpose, and to see 'p. 41'." She does not look at the logbook on her shelf, the one that fell open at page forty on your first day and has a stub where the next page should be. She does not need to.

Maryam, at the bookshop, has been reading the notebook's margins over your shoulder. He has copied out lines of poetry, with a page reference each time. "That's not a notebook habit. That's a library habit. He was reading one particular book." The catalogue has it: an 1898 translation of a Persian poet, borrowed forty years ago, returned eleven years later, without a word and without a fine.

Mrs. Shehu lets you handle it in the rare books room, in gloves that have a sign-out sheet of their own. ("Name. Time. Left glove. Right glove. Sign.") The book opens by itself to a place it has been opened many times. Folded inside is a sheet of logbook paper.

Page forty-one. It is the ring, drawn whole for the only time: six coils, ten small seals around a door, and in the centre a seventh circle that is not a coil. Beside it, in the professor's quick hand: **"Keeper. Must be someone who will leave."** And under that, smaller, in pencil, pressed hard: "Not him. He never will."

Nobody says anything. Shamsu counts something on his fingers and does not tell you what. Maryam offers yam, quietly, which is how you know she is shaken.

You bring the page to Dr. Okonkwo. She fits it against the stub in the logbook. It matches. The cut edge is perfectly straight: a neat blade, a steady hand, a person who could not bear to tear it. You know whose hand cuts that straight. You have watched it trim wire for four years.

You do not say so, and nor does she. She closes the logbook.

"Page forty-one," she says, "is not on the syllabus either."

#### M099 · Fresher Tax, Reversed
**When:** Y5 · S1 Wk 3 · Sat (weekend) · 16:00. **Where:** sports: court. **Who:** Bolaji, Big Baby, Hassan, Abbas, the new "boss", freshers. **Special helpers (if enabled):** none.
**Connects to:** M7 (the tax he invented); M14 (the chalk circle, the fair spar); M34 (the barber's chair); M65 (his carpenter's hands).
**Mystery:** plants C-SYMBOL (the repainted centre circle has six freshers' handprints round it, and a seventh in the middle that nobody claims).

**Story.** A very large shadow falls across a very small fresher on the court. You know that shadow. You paid it ₦500 in your first week. Except that when you look up, it is the wrong shadow: a second-year called Danlami in a vest two sizes too proud, flanked by two friends who have clearly practised folding their arms. "Fresher tax. **Story.** Rain. Grey rain on a grey road, and the game takes things away from you one at a time: first the music, then the map, then the markers, then the date, until there is only you, walking, and the sound of water in a gutter.

The roof is whole. The windows are clean. Six students are at six benches, each building, each alone, each certain. You can walk among them and speak, and they answer without looking up, the way busy people do. One is winding and counting under her breath. One is arguing with a drawing. On the wall the clock says a little before six.

There are seven stools. On the seventh sits a young man in a white shirt, exactly as you know him, with his hands on his knees, watching them work. None of the six looks at him. Not once. They step round the stool as you would step round a post.

He looks at *you*. He seems, for the first time, worn thin, like a shirt washed too often. "I'm a bit tired today. Sorry." He nods at the benches. "You're nearly there. It was never the windings."

You ask him what it was. He smiles, and looks at the sixth bench.

The sixth student has his back to you. Thin, quick hands; a blade laid exactly parallel to the edge of the bench. He turns round to reach for something, and he has the Maker's face. Young. No grey in it. No tiredness. He looks straight through you at the seventh stool, and frowns slightly, as you do when you have forgotten a word.

You wake. The rain is slackening. Kaka is shelling groundnuts as if you had been gone a minute. "Now you've seen it," she says, "I don't have to tell it." She will not say more. Asked about your five-year quest she offers one comment: "You people and your wires. Eat."

There is a letter under the groundnut bowl. The postman left it, she says; it has been redirected four times, from the department to the hostel to the lodge to "the forest side, ask the old woman". The handwriting is brisk and slants uphill.

*Mine burned too. Twice. Ask them for help. All of them. — A.N., still not dead.*

The rain stops. The markers come back, one at a time, shyly. The first one to return is the hostel: home.

Tonight's Nulls do not chase. They walk, in gowns, in ones and twos, and what they touch is gone: a streetlamp, a signpost, the white line on the road, the *name* of a place on your map. Ride round them, and where they have blanked a junction, trust whoever is behind you. The Plug is giving boosts free at both branches: Zobo Max, Bike Nitro, Full Tank, Turbo Sneakers. **The Garden.** Held by the people who helped light it. Bolaji has rolled the barber's chair into the garden gateway as a barricade and sits in it, enormous, arms folded, a towel round his neck. Baba Porter is reading names off the hall register, floor by floor, at the Nulls, who sit down one after another looking embarrassed. Mai Shayi has the kettle on. Abbas is at the coil with a spanner. Your part is the relight you did in Year 1, done fast: three switches in sequence, then the hum.

**The market.** By keke, Shamsu clinging on. Under the mall, coil two; above it, an atrium filling with Nulls who drift up the escalators toward the roof lights. Mubarak has reversed every escalator, so they glide up serenely and arrive, serenely, where they started. Hajiya Ladi has the market women calling names by stall, by family, by debt. If you reported Aliyu in Year 2 he is absent and you work the changeover yourself, harder; if you gave him his chance he is at the breaker in a helmet: "Properly. This time."

And at the loading bay, in pyjamas, with a silk dressing gown and his own generator on a trolley, stands Chief Megawatt. He has pushed it himself. He is out of breath. "I am being emotional," he says. "Don't look at me. Where do I plug it?"

**The forest.** On foot, because no engine goes in. Lawal's patrol holds the path in a chain of torches: "Follow my light." Kaka is at the Old Site door with a frying pan, and a Null in front of her that has plainly decided against it. Jega has run the forest path on a knee that was once in pieces, to stand the six stools up again where they were knocked down. The seventh is on its side. He does not touch it. The coil wants its three notes sung; there are no children here at one in the morning, so the patrol sings, badly, and Lawal sings well, and looks at you as if daring you to mention it.

Three points alight on the map.

And from the cliff above the dam, from the top of the half-built tower, a light is flashing: long, short, long. The first lamp you ever built, on the ladder that ran out of wood. Somebody is up there signalling that the way to the water is clear.

Shamsu squints. "Who is that?"

You honestly cannot tell.

#### M113 · Two by Sky and Water
**When:** Y5 · S2 Wk 12 · Sat · 03:00–05:00. **Where:** phys: the Old Eye; dam: pier, spillway, underwater. **Who:** Prof. Bello, AB, Tanko; Baba Dam, Firdausi, the eel. **(switch: you and Firdausi)** **Special helpers (if enabled):** none.
**Connects to:** M81 (one star, one window; "Left. Left. Check."); M90 (her rhythm, in threes); M16 (the eel remembers the kilishi); M6 (Tanko's riddles); M84 (Baba Dam's boat, and what is varnished to it).
**Mystery:** plants C-TANKO (a straight answer, and one sentence about why he knows the stair in the dark); plants C-SACK (the empty sack marked T is folded on the annex shelf, and tonight it is under his arm).

**Story.** The dome of the Old Eye grinds open on nothing. Not cloud. Cloud has edges and moves. This is a lid.

Prof. Bello stands under the slit with his hands behind his back, looking up at the place where his one star should be crossing. "I said the sky does not reschedule." He clears his throat. "I would like to be proved wrong. Once."

Coil four wakes by starlight or not at all. The window was 01:10 and it is past three. Shamsu, on the phone from the garden, has done the sum that says there is a second star, fainter, that crosses the same slit at 03:40, if the mirror is turned eleven degrees and the lid over the sky has a hole in it.

It has holes. The lantern line is still burning below, a long thread of lights across the campus, and wherever the line is thick the dark above it is thin. You are on the annex roof with the mirror; AB is at the eyepiece below. You cannot see the star at all. He can, in flashes. So it is his voice in your ear, exactly as before, and your hands on the cold brass: "Left. Left. Hold. Left. …Check."

On the third try the lid closes over the gap, and you lose it. The mirror needs to come round by a back stair you never found in M81, in the dark, now.

Tanko has been sitting on the bottom step with a folded sack under his arm, saying nothing, for once without a riddle. You ask him which way. All your years here he has answered "Which?" to that.

"Third door. Up nine. Duck at the beam; it has been waiting forty years to hit somebody. Then the handle is on your left, low. I carried tea up there every night for a year. I know it better than my own name." A pause. "Go. Riddle later."

Nine steps. Duck. Left, low. The mirror swings. AB: "Check. Check! CHECK!" A thread of old light comes down the tube and the fourth coil wakes with a sound like a held breath let go. Prof. Bello takes off his glasses and cleans them for much longer than glasses need.

Then the dam, at a run.

Baba Dam has the boat out already; he does not hurry, and he is somehow there first. The spillway is black, the water is high, and Nulls stand along the dam wall in gowns, looking down at their own reflections with great contentment.

Switch to Firdausi on the bank. She is knee-deep with a paddle, tapping the eel's rhythm on the surface: three, pause, three. Too fast and the eel sulks; too slow and she does not hear. Switch back: you dive, Iron Lungs Gum from the Plug in your cheek, down the spillway's throat to the housing, where the fifth coil sits cold for the first time since she kept it warm.

You are halfway back up, lungs burning, when the dark water under you turns solid. The eel comes up beneath the boat and the boat rises, and she carries you (you, Baba Dam, the lamp, all of it) the length of the spillway like a tide, and sets you down at the switch as gently as a mother putting down a sleeping child.

"She remembers the kilishi," Baba Dam says. "Fish don't hurry. But they don't forget."

You close the switch. Five points alight on the map, a ring with a gap.

In the middle, the clock. Dark.

#### M120 · Fresher
**When:** some years later · a resumption Monday · 07:00. **Where:** gate: ring road, main gate; hostel: three doors, the hall front. **Who:** you; an old man feeding birds; Baba Porter; three new freshers; Muhammad; {{ROOMIE}} (by text). **Special helpers (if enabled):** none.

**Story.** An engine, morning light, and a road you know by heart.

Some years later. The game does not say how many. You are back for a day, as Baba Porter said you would be, on whatever you love best: the okada, the keke, the bicycle from your first ten minutes, the white-and-gold one if you bought it. One last ride round the ring road, with nothing to do and no marker on the map, past everything you lit.

The New Site road, forty lamps, all on in daylight because somebody forgot, and nobody minds. The half-tower on the cliff; it has one more rung. The mall, with a small brass plaque by the door the same size as Mama Nkechi's. The Twin Theatres, full; a fresher running, late, toward the wrong one. The Plug Store, with a third branch. The pitch. The hospital, with a new wing; a cat on the garden wall.

The Amana Clock. It is right. You check it against your phone anyway, like your father.

On a bench in the Faculty garden an old man in ordinary clothes is feeding birds from a paper cone. He is in no hurry at all. He looks up and waves, a small wave, the wave of someone with all day. If you stop, he will walk with you a little way, slowly, and give you one saying, and name nothing. Then he goes back to his birds. He does not come to the gate.

At the hall, three freshers are moving in. The game gives you one minute with each, from a distance, and no captions. One is arguing, brilliantly, with a porter about a room allocation. One is sitting very neatly on a suitcase, reading the handbook. And one has dropped everything to chase a hen that has got out of somebody's car, down the corridor, round the water tank, losing a slipper, and is now carrying it back, apologising to it.

You have a square of paper in your pocket. You have had it for years. Choose a door. Any of the three: the game takes your choice and never tells you if it was right. Slip the note under. You know the words. You drew the six dots and the seventh from memory.

Then the gate, because you are leaving and it is the way out.

A bus pulls in. A young man gets down from it with one bag and an admission letter, and stands looking about him like someone who has never seen any of it: the gate, the road, the clock, the sky. He sees you looking. He comes over.

White shirt. Clean shoes.

"Excuse me. Sorry. Which way is the Senate?"

He does not know you. There is nothing in his face but a fresher's nerves. You tell him. You hear yourself add it: "Don't take the shortcut." He thanks you, twice, and goes, bag bumping his knee.

From the hall door, Baba Porter's voice, older, exactly as loud: "You! Room 606. Your post has been waiting."

You look up at the hall. You count. You could swear it had five floors.

Your phone buzzes. {{ROOMIE}}, that second, from wherever in the world they are: *Riddle me this: what has six floors now? CALL ME.*

You look back at the road. He is small already, walking toward the Senate the long way round, the right way.

"Welcome, fresher."

Cut to black. The clock strikes seven.
